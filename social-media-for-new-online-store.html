Social Media for a New Online Store: Where to Start With Zero Followers

October 30, 2026 5 min read VidyBack Team

You launched the store. The products are listed, the photos look decent, and then you open Instagram and see an account with eleven followers, six of whom are your relatives. Posting into that silence feels pointless. Why spend an evening on a video when nobody is there to watch it?

That feeling is the reason most new stores post for two weeks, then quietly stop. It's worth looking at what's actually going on, because the fix is smaller than you'd think.

The Misconception: You Need an Audience Before You Start

Most advice about social media for a new online store assumes you're building a following first and selling second. So you chase follower counts, follow-for-follow tricks and giveaways, and you end up with a crowd that has no interest in what you sell.

A new store doesn't need a big audience. It needs a small number of the right people to find a consistent, believable account and think, "this is a real shop, and I can see what they sell." Social profiles work a lot like a shop window on a quiet street. Someone who has already heard of you, or clicked an ad, or landed on your product page, will often check your Instagram before they buy. An account with recent, regular posts reassures them. An empty one, or one that last posted in March, does the opposite.

So the first goal isn't reach. It's looking active and legitimate, and that's something you can build with a few posts a week.

Pick Two Platforms, Not Five

The fastest way to burn out is trying to be everywhere in week one. Start with two. A reasonable pairing for most product stores is Instagram plus one of TikTok or Pinterest, depending on what you sell.

  • Instagram is where people tend to check a store before buying, so it works as your public storefront.
  • TikTok suits products that look good moving or being used: kitchen gadgets, skincare, fashion, pet items.
  • Pinterest suits visual, giftable or planning-heavy products like home decor, stationery and craft supplies. Pins keep getting found for a long time after you post them.

You can add Facebook and YouTube later once the routine feels easy. If you're torn between the first two, our comparison of Instagram vs TikTok for a small online store goes through it in more detail.

A Starter Plan for the First Four Weeks

Here's a schedule a one-person store can actually keep. It's deliberately modest: four posts a week on your main platform, plus a repost or two on the second one.

Week 1: Set up the profile so it explains itself

Before you post anything, spend an hour on the basics. Use your logo or a clean product photo as the profile image. Write a bio that says what you sell and who it's for in one line, for example "Handmade soy candles for small apartments. Ships from Ohio." Add your store link. Post your first three or four items: a product shot, a short clip of the product in use, and a simple "here's who we are" post.

Weeks 2 and 3: Show the products, one at a time

Pick your four best sellers or favourite listings and give each its own post. Not a collage, not a sale announcement. One product, one clear angle: how big it is, what it's made of, where it ends up in someone's day. Short product videos of 15 to 30 seconds tend to be the easiest format, because you can film one in a few minutes with a phone.

For captions, say the useful thing plainly. Size, material, price, who it suits. If you're stuck, the formula in our post on Instagram captions for online store products will get you through a month.

Week 4: Mix in the human stuff

Once the product posts are in place, add two or three that show the shop behind them: how you pack an order, where you source materials, why you started. These don't need to be polished. They give a stranger a reason to trust you with their card details.

Details That Save You Time

Hashtags: use a handful, not thirty. Three to five relevant ones is plenty on Instagram, such as one for your product type, one for your niche and one for your location if you sell locally. If you want to check a caption before you post, the free hashtag counter counts them for you.

Posting times: don't lose a week researching the perfect hour. Pick consistent slots, say Tuesday, Thursday and Saturday at 6pm local time, and keep them for a month. Consistency matters more than the exact minute.

Batching: film all four of your product clips in one sitting, then schedule them. Doing it in a single session means you only set up lighting and a clean background once.

Expectations: in the first month, a post might get 20 views. That's normal. You're not trying to go viral. You're building a feed that looks like a real shop, so that the next person who finds you has something to scroll through.

When Posting by Hand Stops Being Realistic

This plan works fine at 10 products. At 50 or 100, it gets heavy.
