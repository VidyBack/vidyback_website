How Often Should a Small Online Store Post on Facebook? A Realistic Schedule

October 9, 2026 5 min read VidyBack Team

You open Facebook, see that your store's Page hasn't had a new post in nine days, and feel that small flicker of guilt. Then you search "how often should a small business post on Facebook" and get answers ranging from "once a day" to "twice a day" to "it depends." None of them mention that you also run the store, pack the orders and answer the emails.

So the Page sits there, quietly going stale, while Instagram and TikTok get whatever energy is left over.

The Misconception: Facebook Needs a Daily Feed

Most posting-frequency advice is written for media companies and agencies with a person whose whole job is the feed. A small online store isn't that. You don't need to post on Facebook the way a news site does, and trying to will mostly burn you out within a month.

There's no magic number that Facebook rewards. What matters far more is that your Page looks alive and current when someone lands on it. Think about how people actually reach a small store's Facebook Page: they click through from an ad, from a link in a group, or after Googling your store name to see if you're legit. The first thing they check is the most recent post. If it's from March, they wonder whether you're still open.

A Realistic Facebook Posting Frequency for a Small Store

Here's a sensible way to think about it, based on how much time you honestly have:

  • Minimum that still looks active: 2 posts a week. Enough that the top of your Page is never more than a few days old. This is the floor, not the goal.
  • A comfortable middle: 3 to 4 posts a week. Enough variety to mix new products, restocks and the occasional behind-the-scenes clip without repeating yourself.
  • Ambitious but only worth it if it's automated: 5 or more a week. Fine, but only if you're not creating each post by hand.

Consistency beats volume here. Three posts a week for six months does more for a Page than a burst of daily posting for two weeks followed by silence. If you've read our piece on why posting 5 to 7 times a week beats posting once with perfect content, the same logic applies: a steady rhythm of good-enough posts wins over rare polished ones.

What to Actually Post (When You're Not a Content Person)

Frequency is only half the problem. The other half is staring at a blank text box wondering what to say. For a store with a product catalog, you already have most of the raw material:

  1. New arrivals. A short video or a clean photo of the product, a sentence on who it's for, and the link. A candle shop might post "The new cedar and fig soy candle is up, 40-hour burn time, hand-poured in small batches."
  2. Back in stock. These are surprisingly useful, because someone has probably been waiting. One line and a link is plenty.
  3. One product, one specific detail. The stitching on a leather wallet, the fabric weight of a tee, how a planter drains. Specifics feel more human than "shop now."
  4. Simple how-to or use ideas. Three ways to style a scarf, or what fits in a 20 litre backpack.
  5. Shipping and policy reminders. Holiday cut-off dates, free shipping thresholds, returns window. Dull to write, but they answer questions before customers have to ask.

Notice what's missing: anything that needs a studio, a script or a morning of editing. Short, specific, product-led posts are enough.

Video or Photos on Facebook?

Both work, and mixing them keeps the Page from feeling repetitive. If you have to choose, short vertical video tends to be the better use of a product when it shows something photos can't: movement, scale, texture, how it unfolds or packs down. Photos are better for simple, clean product shots and for announcements where the text carries the message.

The practical bonus is that a short vertical product video you make for Instagram Reels or TikTok can usually go on Facebook too. You don't need a separate Facebook-only creative workflow. If you want a fuller walkthrough of reusing one clip, see one product video, five platforms.

Best Time to Post on Facebook

You'll see lots of confident charts claiming the perfect hour. Take them with a grain of salt: your audience in Manchester or Toronto won't behave like a generic average. A better approach is to start with times when people are likely to be on their phones, such as lunch breaks and early evenings in your main customer time zone, and keep it consistent. Then notice how your own audience responds on the Page itself, through comments and messages, and adjust. Don't agonize over it. A post that goes out at a decent time is far better than one that never goes out because you were waiting for the perfect slot.

Batching Is the Part That Makes It Sustainable

The reason most small stores fall off on Facebook isn't strategy, it's the daily friction. Every post means picking a product, finding the files, writing a caption, logging in and publishing. Doing that three or four times a week is a real tax.

The fix is to batch. Set aside one block a week, even 30 minutes, to line up the next several posts, then schedule them. Facebook's own scheduling tools can handle this if you're posting by hand. The one-time work of choosing which products to feature and getting the content ready is where most of the effort lives.
